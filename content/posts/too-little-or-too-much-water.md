Science fiction is ripe with alien species from planets that are far wetter than Earth, or far drier and hotter. Whether it's the sandworms of *Dune* or the Mon Calamari (think Admiral Ackbar), I don't think it's likely we'll find pre-interstellar civilizations originating on planets that are giant deserts or on ocean worlds.

Although we've found, time and time again, that life can adapt to incredibly extreme climates, the hardy survivors we've found have adapted to the new climates and did not originate in them. Life on Earth originated in our soupy seas, where early single-celled organisms could eat nutrients and paddle around with little hairs, occasionally splitting in half to reproduce.

## Deserts

On a desert planet it would be very difficult for early life to arise (and it would be more vulnerable should it initially arise in isolated bodies of water), propagate, and evolve into more advanced life. The alien equivalent of cavemen would have more trouble starting early fires and building early cities. While intelligent life could theoretically overcome these challenges, it would be far more difficult for life to originate in the first place.

## Ocean worlds

The opposite is true of water worlds. While life would have a much easier time coming about, I think that the conditions of aquatic life bias towards larger, faster and more physical animals as opposed to more intelligent animals, like early hominids and the precursors to pre-interstellar civilizations.

In Kurt Vonnegut's *Galápagos*, people evolve more slender heads to be able to swim more efficiently, gaining in speed at the cost of brain size. Even though respectable intelligence has evolved in dolphins and whales, the lack of grasping hands (which would represent a sacrifice of speed and maneuverability) limits their ability to capitalize on their larger brains. Without grasping hands, whales and dolphins will never be able to develop simple tools, writing, or eventually, spaceships.
