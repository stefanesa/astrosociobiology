As much as I like the Mon Calamari, and as much as octopi might seem sentient, I think it exceedingly unlikely that advanced aliens might be aquatic in nature. And given the immense evolutionary pressures towards specialization, I think it's similarly unlikely advanced aliens would be amphibious.

There are just too many ways that being underwater makes it harder to build something that looks like an advanced society.

## Fire, hands, and records

Take fire, for example. While it is obvious that advanced octopi won't be able to have campfires on the barrier reef, the bigger consequence is that without being able to sterilize food through heat, it will be exceedingly hard for an aquatic species to build the sorts of population numbers that beget early society. Too many will simply die of infections and parasites.

Furthermore, in the water (or any liquid, for that matter) dexterity and mobility are diametrically opposed goals. Once early hominid ancestors descended from the trees they suddenly had arms with little use for mobility, and lots of ability to carry stuff. Handed dolphins, however, would likely just be really bad swimmers (which would put pressure on their hands to flipperize).

While you could, theoretically, have an intellectually capable species without hands or fire, my suspicion is they won't be able to build what I'd consider an advanced society (defined again as one capable of sending matter or information beyond their solar system).

I suspect early inventions like fire and pointy sticks kick off a positive feedback loop for invention. The minds that improve stick sharpening in one generation might, a few thousand generations later, form limited government and, a few generations after that, design global telecommunications networks capable of sharing cat-analog pictures with each other and with their solar neighbors.

What's more, an aquatic species might find it incredibly hard to find early breakthroughs in recording their data. It's really hard to keep ink in one place underwater, and the relative feeling of weightlessness, I suspect, would make chiseling commandments into stone incredibly burdensome.

Last but not least, having any hope of leaving your planet from underwater would be incredibly hard (and, to think, forging the steel needed for such attempts would be very difficult underwater).

## The valedictorian in the basement

This set of limitations presents an interesting quandary: what's to be said of the aliens that are more advanced than microorganisms but are unable to reach an advanced stage? There are of course those we might recognize as animals, like a deer or lizard analog, but what of the species who have nearly all the things they need to become advanced (the intelligence, the relative mastery of their local ecosystem) but are limited by their evolutionary heritage of, say, being aquatic and possessing flippers?

In a way we could think of these species as similar to the valedictorian who, despite their great promise, is still living in their parents' basement at 40.

What fascinates me most about this direction of inquiry is that we might have species like this on Earth, and they might be some of our closest opportunities to learn about the choices alien species might make.

I suspect you could separate species like this into three groups:

- those who have not yet advanced to the stage to be "advanced," like Stone Age man, the Ewoks or, I suspect, raccoons;
- those whose choices close the path to them, like whales or dolphins; and
- those where the evolutionary path is uncertain, like bonobos or octopi.

Which is all good, until a raccoon points a spear at me during trash night.
