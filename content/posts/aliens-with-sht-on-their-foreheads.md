> "You know, Worf from *Star Trek*, how he has all that sh*t all over his face, but he's just a human in a costume." — *Rick and Morty*, S02E08

It is a bit of a trope in lower-budget sci-fi to make aliens just be humans with prosthetics on their foreheads (or sometimes, on their ears). It makes a lot of sense when you need to convince an actor to spend 100 days for 10 years getting dressed up the same way, but does it make sense in real life?

Defenders of Klingon forehead ridges (or Elvish ears) might say that nature has plenty of non-utilitarian features. Baboons have prominent noses (and derrieres), lions have manes, peacocks have, well, you get the picture.

Compared to that, human faces are downright plain. And I don't just think I'm xenocentric.

## The low cost of a human face

Human faces, without makeup or grooming, have very few features that seem dedicated to differentiation. There is certainly variation in low-calorie-difference features like hairline, nose shape, eye color, facial hair, ears, and pigmentation, but there is nothing so prominent as to lead to bone adornment. Next to a Klingon, our faces look downright utilitarian.

I suspect much of this has to do with how humans, as opposed to peacocks, compete for mates. Peacocks are competing on feathers as a proxy for health and ability to gather sufficient food. Humans, on the other hand, have spent the last 100,000 years competing based on how good we are at using our intelligence and ingenuity as a way of demonstrating value to mates.

And I think there's a good chance these are causally linked. If the easiest way to reproduce was to have a particularly flashy flap of skin you could expand from under your chin, you probably wouldn't need to waste time studying for the SATs. Or, you know, designing the trebuchet.

I would note that other intelligent terrestrial species do not seem to have loud adornment. Blue whales, orcas, chimps et al. are "relatively utilitarian," and seem to use their intelligence to differentiate themselves to prospective mates.

There is probably, in fact, a relatively low caloric budget for adornment you can have and still be a species intelligent enough to become an "interesting alien."

## They might look like us

If this is true, it could have massive implications for the intelligent aliens we might meet: they are highly unlikely to have biologically derived differentiation such as prominent forehead folds, gender-derived flashing flaps of skin, musk glands, etc.

They might look like, to throw a crazy idea out there, us.

Human physical development was shaped by our particular family tree of predecessor species: some group of apes that spread to the grasslands, eventually self-domesticated, and built a civilization. Another species of intelligent aliens that evolved from a similarly pressured family tree, in a similar environment, might look 90% like humans. I have no doubt that the remaining 10% might seem shocking (maybe they have blue skin and ears that are 10% larger!!) but I doubt they are identical save some forehead ridges, or as alien as a six-foot-tall, color-changing, feather-adorned peacock-man.

I also wouldn't be incredibly surprised if, upon meeting such a species, we could make a better-than-even guess at the type of environment they evolved in. Is their planet relatively cold, or relatively warm? Relatively dry or relatively wet?
