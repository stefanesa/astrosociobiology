Many of the most terrifying antagonist intelligent aliens are obligate carnivores. The idea of aliens that not only have better guns than us, but eat us when they use them on us, is one that many a writer has clung to. But is it feasible?

## The calorie problem

In biology, organisms have about a 10% efficiency rate when converting calories they eat into calories they use. That means that if 1,000 units of energy come from the sun, a blade of grass captures 100 units of it, the cow captures 10 units of that, and only 1 unit is captured by the person eating the cow.

If you had a population of 7 billion obligate carnivores, you would need a lot of cows.

At this point in human society, we can support that bovine population. But in prehistory that would have been important. Most predators in the wild eat less frequently than once a day, meaning they have to spend much more of their time hunting than early humans spent gathering food. They also need larger, exclusive ranges than it seems humans may have needed. It doesn't take much human population to hunt an area clear of large prey animals.

Meat also doesn't transit particularly well. The Roman Empire could be thought of, in a way, as a bunch of slackers living off of Egyptian grain farmers. I doubt as many calories of cow / sheep / pig could have crossed the Mediterranean.

## The problem with pure herbivores

On the other hand, it's unclear if an obligate vegetarian species could become intelligent aliens.

Most intelligent obligate vegetarians seem to be so because they're so big that nothing can eat them. When your strategy for survival becomes mass (blue whales, elephants), throwing a few dozen extra pounds into the brain seems like an easy investment.

It seems incredibly hard for an elephant-sized species to ever build an early rocket ship.

But at a far more basic level, it might be hard for such a species to have access to an appendage they can use to do stuff. Elephant trunks are amazing, but they seem ill-equipped to build even an early shelter for an elephant, much less the sorts of early bridges that often seem like the spark-stone for greater engineering.

Outside of trunks, part of why it seems so hard for an elephantine species to have hands and thumbs is that, by the time they're that big, their locomotion decisions are almost certainly locked in. You could have a giant-sloth-looking creature, but evolving from primarily four-legged motion to standing upright could take longer than humans have been separated from other large great apes.

## Hunting as a primer for civilization

Finally, eating meat is a really good species primer for purposely killing predators. It is hard to see a species take over its planet (much less its solar system) if it only ever kills its predators when they choose to attack. The skills humans learned hunting deer were then used to kill the tiger that had been eating their neighbors. This same set of skills begets warfare, which might be one of the big civilizational primers for engineering and urbanization, without which a species probably never puts satellites in orbit.
